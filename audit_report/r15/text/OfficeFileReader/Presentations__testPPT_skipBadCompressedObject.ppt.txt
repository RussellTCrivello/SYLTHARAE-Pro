October 10, 2000

NASA Human Information Research and Technology Division

An Evaluation of CDTI Dynamic Predictor Display Technology

Walter W. Johnson, Lisa Thomas, and Vernol Battiste

Human Information Research and Technology Division

NASA Ames Research Center

Background

Free Flight proposes to place some or all of the responsibility for conflict resolutions in the hands of the flight crew

In order to effectively perform this function the flight crew will need displays and tools that aid in evaluating proposed resolutions

Purpose

Compare and contrast the relative utility of two CDTI-based tools for resolving conflicts

Dynamic Conflict Alerting

Dynamic Trajectory Pulse Prediction

Design

Manipulations

3 Scenario Variables

Conflict Angle

Initial Ownship Distance from Conflict

Initial Traffic Distance from Conflict

2 Display Variables

Conflict Scenarios

Near  (2/3 Ownship Distance/Speed)

Mid  (1.0 x Ownship Distance/Speed)

Far    (1.5 x Ownship Distance/Speed)

Traffic

Design (cont.)

Dependent Variables

Response Time

Resolution Conflict Probability (how often resolution worked)

Distance to Traffic at Closest Approach

Cost of Resolution (path deviation size in miles)

Subjects

8 General Aviation Pilots

Procedure

Trials were blocked by Dynamic Conflict Alerting and Dynamic Trajectory Pulse Prediction

Each block showed all combinations of Conflict Angle, Initial Ownship Distance-to-Conflict, and Initial Traffic Distance-to-Conflict

Subjects were instructed to solve each conflict with a primary emphasis on safety and a secondary emphasis on efficiency

Summary

Loss of Separation (LOS):

Tools to avoid LOS were needed for conflict angles of 60 degrees or less.

Dynamic alerting was the most effective aid for avoiding LOS.

Closest Approach Distance:

Dynamic alerting led to substantially smaller minimum separations

Minimum separations were the least at the largest and smallest conflict angles.

Head-on encounters(large angle) can be resolved with only a path separation at the conflict point.

Small conflict angles require larger and less simple deviations.  An attempt to minimize this deviation could lead to the observed closer approaches and the greater number of projected LOS events.

Resolution Costs:

Dynamic alerting was the most effective aid for creating efficient resolutions

With dynamic alerting, resolution costs monotonically decreased with conflict angle

Without dynamic alerting, resolution costs rose to a peak at 60 degrees and then fell.  These smaller path deviations were correlated with higher conflict probabilities

For intercept angles less than 60 degrees, dynamic trajectory prediction led pilots to generate resolutions with larger deviations (even when dynamic alerting was present)

Response Time:

Dynamic alerting led to faster response times

Response times increased as conflict angle decreased

Summary (cont.)

Conclusions

There is a need for aiding only for intercept angles less than 90 degrees.

The lack of any aiding led to substantially inferior performance for intercept angles less than 90 degrees

Alerting was the most effective aid for avoiding LOS.  The addition of trajectory pulse prediction was not of value.

Trajectory pulse prediction was important for avoiding LOS only when alerting was not present

Alerting led to substantially smaller separations overall

Approximately equal separations both with and without alerting were found for the smallest and the largest angles.  This may be due to different mechanisms.  For the near head-on encounters (large angles), path separation at the conflict point will be the largest separation.  For the low conflict angles this is not the case and too small of a deviation will bring the aircraft close where their paths cross.

Substantially larger separations were generated when resolving more lateral conflicts (those closer to 90 degrees)

Smaller conflict angles require larger deviations, and the desire to minimize the size of the deviations would lead to closer approaches for smaller conflict angles

Conflict angles greater than 90 degrees require smaller deviations

Alerting led to faster response times

Response times were approximately monotonic with conflict angle, with longer response times for more acute intercept angles

Pulse prediction led to longer response times for the most acute intercept angles when no alerting was present.  This was probably due to the difficult nature of the solution, and the time needed to make effective use of the pulse prediction
